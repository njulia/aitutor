What are 11+ acceptance rates?

Definition

The 11+ acceptance rate is the percentage of applicants who are offered a place at a selective school. It can be calculated as:

Acceptance rate = (places offered ÷ applicants) × 100

Why rates vary by school

About 5–15%

Super-selective schools

Often found in London and the South East, these schools may receive five to ten applications for every available place.

About 15–25%

Highly selective schools

Competition remains strong, with somewhat more places relative to the number of applicants.

About 30–50%

Selective regional schools

Schools in some regions may have more places available relative to local application numbers.

About 50–80%

Less competitive selective schools

These may serve lower-density areas. Entry is still selective and requires a strong performance.

What the percentage means

A 5% acceptance rate does not mean only one in twenty children is capable. It means the school received many more applications than it had places, so offers went to the highest-ranked applicants in that cohort.

A 50% acceptance rate still describes a selective process. It usually means there are more places relative to applications, not that the learning or teaching is automatically weaker.

i

Application numbers, priority areas, sibling rules, pupil-premium arrangements and distance criteria can all affect the apparent acceptance rate.

What do these numbers really tell you?

Acceptance rate is not the same as school quality

Misconception

“A school with a 10% acceptance rate must be better than one with a 40% rate.”

Reality

A lower rate may reflect location, reputation, catchment arrangements or applicant volume. Both schools could provide excellent teaching and support.

The value-added story

Student A · Super-selective school

  • 11+ entry: 99th percentile
  • GCSE outcome: mostly Grades 8–9
  • Measured value added may look smaller because the starting point was already very high.

Student B · Selective school

  • 11+ entry: 92nd percentile
  • GCSE outcome: mostly Grades 7–9
  • Measured value added may appear larger because the pupil made more progress from the starting point.

Understanding GCSE performance data

1. Pass rate · Grade 4+

The percentage of pupils achieving a standard pass. The source guide suggests many grammar schools report roughly 92–99%, but current figures must be checked.

2. Strong pass · Grade 7+

The proportion achieving higher grades. This can be more useful than a basic pass rate when comparing high-attaining cohorts.

3. Progress 8

A measure of progress between the end of primary school and GCSEs. Very high-attaining pupils can create a “ceiling effect”, so interpret the score in context.

4. Entry-to-GCSE stability

Whether pupils broadly maintain their relative position. A stable high-performing cohort may show less variation than a broader intake.

i

Compare the same measure, year and subject coverage. A whole-school pass rate and a selected-subject Grade 7+ rate are not like-for-like.

How to use this information for preparation

Identify target schools

Research two or three realistic options. Include a reach choice, a target choice and a more likely choice where possible. Read each school’s current admissions policy.

Assess the current level

Use a diagnostic paper to review Maths, English, Verbal Reasoning and Non-Verbal Reasoning, depending on the actual test format.

Create a realistic target

Use current standardisation and familiarisation information from the school or test provider. Raw marks do not transfer reliably between different papers.

Focus the preparation

Match practice to the target school’s subjects, question style, timing and delivery format rather than simply choosing the most famous paper.

Track steady progress

Record accuracy, timing and repeated mistakes. Use the information to choose the next small area to practise.

Beyond acceptance rates: what else matters?

📍 Location and transport

A long daily journey affects sleep, homework time, friendships and family life. Test the real commute at school-travel time.

🎓 Sixth-form outcomes

Review current A-level subject choice, outcomes and destinations, but remember these are several years away for an 11+ applicant.

🤝 Pastoral care and enrichment

Ask how the school supports wellbeing, additional needs and different learning styles. Explore clubs, trips, music and sport.

🌱 Fit for the child

Consider personality, motivation, confidence and resilience. The strongest school on paper is not automatically the best environment for every child.

Common questions answered

If my child is at the 95th percentile and a school has a 10% acceptance rate, will they get in?

Answer: It may place them in a competitive range, but it is not a guarantee. Offers depend on the full cohort, standardisation, eligibility, admissions rules and how each part of the test is weighted.

Is it better to aim for a 50% school or try for a 20% school?

Answer: Both can lead to excellent outcomes. Balance ambition with stress, travel, school culture and the environment in which the child is most likely to thrive.

Why can results vary between highly selective schools?

Answer: Cohort size, subject entry patterns and reporting methods can differ. Always compare the same year, measure and subject coverage.

My child is in Year 4. How should acceptance rates affect preparation?

Answer: Use rates only as context. Focus first on strong foundations, reading, vocabulary, number fluency and calm problem-solving. Increase exam-specific practice gradually after checking the actual target-school format.

Key points to remember

  1. Acceptance rate is one metric, not a measure of how “good” a school is.
  2. GCSE outcomes reflect both intake and education, so compare progress as well as attainment.
  3. Fit matters: travel, wellbeing, culture and support can matter more than prestige.
  4. Preparation should match the real test, not a school’s fame.
  5. Success means progress, not only the name on a school badge.

The 11+ is an entry point to secondary education, not its final outcome. Choose a school where the child can learn, belong and grow.
